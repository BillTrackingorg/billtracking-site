Accuracy & corrections

What you can rely on, and what to check

BillTracking is built from official records, and what it posts is copied straight from them. Summaries are the legislatures' own, quoted word for word. This page explains where mistakes can still happen and what we do about them. The rule we build by: a wrong claim is worse than a hedged or missing one.

The rules the system enforces

How posts stay honest

  • Official sources only. Posts are generated from Congress.gov and from the EU's official registers: the European Parliament's open-data service, the Publications Office and the Legislative Observatory (OEIL). They never come from news coverage or social media.
  • Numbers come from the official record. Vote counts, dates and bill numbers are copied exactly as published. If the record has no number, the line is left out rather than estimated.
  • When in doubt, stay silent. If an action in the record is ambiguous, the system logs it and stays silent instead of posting a guess. Ambiguous means a procedural step the system can't confidently classify.
  • Real deadlines only. "The President has ten days (Sundays excepted)" is a constitutional fact, so the feed states it. Trilogue timing is not published, so the feed says that instead of inventing a timetable.
  • Uncertainty stays in words. Where a bill's path could genuinely go more than one way, the feed says so with "expected", "possible" or "if and when". A claim about likelihood always gives its basis. A guess is never presented as a fact.
  • No AI writes what you read. Every post is built from the official record by fixed templates that use the record's own terms. Nothing on a card is written by AI.
  • Summaries are quoted, never written. Each summary is the legislature's own official summary, quoted word for word under its source's name. We never write, shorten or edit one. Spot a summary under the wrong bill or version? Email accuracy@billtracking.org.
  • Non-binding means saying so. When a parliamentary resolution changes no law, the post always says "non-binding". A political agreement is never called a law. And "in force" is never confused with "applies to you now".

Summaries

How summaries work

Each legislature publishes summaries of its own bills: the Congressional Research Service at the Library of Congress for US bills, and the European Parliament's Legislative Observatory for EU files. We show them as published:

  • Word for word. The summary is copied exactly, every paragraph, and labelled with its source and the version or stage it describes, for example "Passed House".
  • Matched to the version. A post carries a summary only when it describes the version of the bill the post's action concerned. When an action changes a bill's text, its post waits for the summary of the new text rather than show the old one.
  • Or none. Official summaries often arrive weeks after a bill moves. Until one exists for the right version, the post goes out without a summary.

What stays the same. A post keeps the summary it went out with. A bill's page shows the summary from its latest post.

Earlier posts. Summaries were once written by AI. Every post on BillTracking that carried one now shows the official summary of the version it concerned, or none where there is none. Copies posted to X keep the summary they went out with, marked [AI-Generated].

When we get it wrong

Corrections are visible, never silent

Automated systems that read government databases will sometimes be wrong. Sometimes that is because of our own bugs. Sometimes it is because the record itself is amended after the fact. We don't promise perfection. We promise that no error is fixed quietly. An erroneous post is corrected in the open. The post itself carries a Corrected flag, the values that changed are marked, and a footer says what it used to say, when it changed and why. On X the correction goes out as a reply to the original post. Every correction is listed in our public corrections log.

Report an error

Spot a post that doesn't match the official record? Email accuracy@billtracking.org with a link to the post and to the official source. Every report is read.

Credit, if you want it. A confirmed report is credited in its log entry if you explicitly opt in, and only then. The credit shows your name or your X handle, whichever you choose. Otherwise, reports are anonymous. Students: a credited correction to a public record is a nice thing to be able to link to. Instructors: there's a ready-made exercise built on exactly this.

Corrections log

Every confirmed correction is listed at billtracking.org/corrections. Each entry shows what the post used to say, what it says now, the date it changed and why. It also shows the source, where a correction has one. That page is generated from the published record itself, so it can't fall behind the posts it describes. When there is nothing to log, it says so plainly.

Reader votes

How the reader vote is counted

Readers can vote Yea or Nay on a bill. It is reader sentiment, not a poll of anyone's electorate. The people who voted are the people who happened to read the post. They are not a sample of anything. The method is published here so a figure taken from this site can be checked against how it was produced.

  • One account, one vote per bill. That is the only reason voting asks you to sign in. It is what stops the same account counting twice. Change your mind and your vote changes. Withdraw it and it leaves the count.
  • Counts are exact. They are never rounded, grouped into ranges or blurred with random noise. A cited number is the real count.
  • Every place inside the bill's own legislature is named, with its count. That means US states on a US bill and member countries on an EU one. There is no minimum group size. None of them is hidden or merged into "other", however few votes it has. Every split shows how many people it is, so "Wyoming, 1 vote, 100% Yea" reads as one person, not as a percentage. Votes from outside that legislature are counted together in one row. Votes with no country go in another. So no vote is dropped.
  • Location is optional, self-reported and coarse. It is never finer than country and US state. It is never verified, and never worked out from your IP address. A place row means "readers who selected Belgium", not "readers in Belgium". Votes with no location still count in the overall total.
  • A vote carries the location you had when you last cast it. The location is recorded on the vote itself. That way, a reader who later moves country does not silently rewrite figures already published.
  • Who voted which way is never published. It is never shown to anyone or attached to a profile. There are no public profiles and no public vote histories. Only counts are published. Deleting your account removes your votes from every future count.

What this cannot promise. In a very small or very lopsided place row, someone who already knows that a particular reader voted could guess how. Publishing any totals at all carries that risk. A minimum group size would move the line, not remove it. That risk is why location is optional in the first place.

How we protect the reader vote

Voting needs an account, and an account needs a Google or Apple sign-in. One account casts one vote per bill. Tapping the other choice moves your vote. Tapping the one you already chose withdraws it. Deleting your account removes every vote you cast.

Counts are exact. We never round them. Every split shows how many people it is, so a one-person row reads as one person.

We keep a daily history of each bill's counts, by place. We also count the votes that leave with deleted accounts. That way a fall in a total can say votes left, rather than people changed their minds.

We don't fingerprint devices. We don't verify the location you give us. And the count we keep of a departed vote identifies nobody.

Two limits worth knowing. One account is one vote, but nothing stops a determined person from creating several accounts across both platforms. What makes that hard is Google's and Apple's own account constraints, not ours. And these are the readers who chose to vote, not a sample of anyone's electorate.
